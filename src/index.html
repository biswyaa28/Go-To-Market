The Company
01 — Company Snapshot

A biochemical company that sells consistency.

  • Standardizes and licenses a universal color-matching language used across design, print and manufacturing.
  • Sells physical guides, digital libraries, licensing and consulting — not paint, not ink, not pigment.
  • Serves print, fashion, textiles, plastics, packaging and product design worldwide.

Target Customer
02 — Who Buys Color

The professional who cannot afford to guess.

Primary

Graphic designers, print production managers, textile & fashion designers and packaging teams, aged roughly 25–55 — people who reference a color code daily in briefs, approvals and purchase orders.

Secondary

Lifestyle merchandise buyers, and brand marketers licensing an exclusive shade of their own — a Tiffany Blue, a UPS Brown.

They don't buy color. They buy certainty.

Customer Problem
03 — The Problem

The same red isn't the same red.

Before Pantone (1958)

Designers and printers relied on vague verbal descriptions, or ad-hoc, in-house matching by individual print shops — inconsistent from vendor to vendor.

The failure mode

One color, rendered across a billboard, a package and a garment, can drift just enough to confuse customers and quietly damage a brand. Ask Kodak about its yellow.

Product Strategy
05 — Product Strategy

One system, three formats.

  1. Universally recognized numbered codes — a shared vocabulary for color, understood the same way in Mumbai or Milan.
  2. Physical fan decks & guides — true, side-by-side matching across print and fabric mediums.
  3. Digital plugins & APIs — embedded directly inside the tools designers already open every day.

13/14
Final Reflection
13 — Final Reflection
Doing exceptionally well

"Color of the Year" — a content strategy with no real peer: one low-cost announcement, worldwide free press, every year.

What we'd change

Pricing accessibility — the cost of official guides and licensing shuts out freelancers, students and smaller businesses in emerging markets.

Pantone's GTM strategy, in one line: make the standard indispensable, then charge for access to it.
